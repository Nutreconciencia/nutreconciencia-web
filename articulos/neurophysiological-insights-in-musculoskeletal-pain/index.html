Abstract

Musculoskeletal pain is a multidimensional phenomenon influenced by neurophysiological processes, as well as psychological and sociocultural context. This chapter examines the foundational basis of pain, highlighting mechanisms such as peripheral and central sensitization, cortical reorganization, and dysfunction in descending inhibitory pathways. Chronic pain is not merely an extension of acute pain but a distinct condition involving maladaptive neuroplasticity, altered sensory processing, and persistent activation of the supraspinal areas. The experience of pain is shaped by individual beliefs, emotional responses, and environmental factors, making it inherently subjective and variable. Avoidance behaviors, fear of movement, and cognitive distortions contribute to pain persistence and reduced quality of life. Clinical features such as allodynia, hyperalgesia, and temporal summation serve as indicators of potential central sensitization, which, even though it is not yet proven in humans, it is the hypothesis for the underlying mechanism of various chronic pain syndromes. Effective management requires a shift from a biomedical model to a biopsychosocial approach, emphasizing patient education, movement-based interventions, and contextual understanding. Rather than seeking to fix dysfunctions, clinicians are encouraged to facilitate recovery by promoting self-efficacy and movement-based strategies. Thus, the purpose of this chapter is to provide an integrative overview of the neurophysiological mechanisms underlying musculoskeletal pain and to translate these concepts into clinically meaningful implications for clinical practice.
